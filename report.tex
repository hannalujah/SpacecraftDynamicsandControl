	\chapter*{Abstract}
	\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
	
	This project presents the development and validation of a complete attitude dynamics and control framework for a nano-satellite operating in low Mars orbit. The mission requires the spacecraft to autonomously transition between three operational pointing modes: Sun-pointing for power generation, nadir-pointing for science observations, and GMO-pointing for communication with a geostationary Mars orbiter.
	
	Reference frames for each mission mode were derived analytically and implemented in MATLAB together with attitude error evaluation and rigid-body rotational dynamics propagation. A proportional-derivative control law was developed to track time-varying reference attitudes and maintain spacecraft stability during mission-mode transitions. The full mission scenario was simulated over a 6500-second orbital timeline with autonomous switching based on illumination and communication visibility conditions.
	
	To complement the numerical simulations, the mission was reconstructed in Systems Tool Kit (STK), providing three-dimensional visual verification of spacecraft orientation throughout the mission. The results demonstrate successful closed-loop stabilization and reliable autonomous transitions between operational modes. Sun-pointing and GMO-pointing objectives were achieved consistently, while the nadir-pointing interval revealed incomplete convergence to the desired Mars-facing orientation. This discrepancy, confirmed by both MATLAB and STK, highlights an area for future controller refinement. Overall, the project validates the integrated attitude-control architecture while demonstrating the value of combining numerical simulation with visual mission reconstruction.
	
	
	\chapter{Introduction}
	
	This project considers a small science satellite orbiting Mars in a Low Mars Orbit (LMO), tasked with collecting scientific data from the Martian surface and relaying the acquired information to a larger mother spacecraft orbiting in a Geostationary Mars Orbit (GMO). In addition to science and communication operations, the spacecraft must periodically orient its solar panels toward the Sun to maintain sufficient onboard power.
	
	Accordingly, the spacecraft mission is divided into three operational modes:
	
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
		\item \textbf{Science Mode:} pointing the sensor toward the Martian surface,
		\item \textbf{Power Mode:} orienting the solar panels toward the Sun,
		\item \textbf{Communication Mode:} pointing the communication antenna toward the GMO spacecraft.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Both the LMO and GMO spacecraft are assumed to follow known circular orbits whose orientations are described using Hill reference frames generated through a 3-1-3 Euler-angle sequence.
	
	The LMO spacecraft is equipped with:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item A science sensor aligned with the $+b_1$ body axis,
		\item Solar panels whose normal vector aligns with the $b_3$ axis,
		\item A communication antenna aligned with the $-b_1$ axis.
	\end{itemize}
	
	The objective of this project is to design and validate an attitude determination and control framework capable of autonomously switching between the required operational pointing modes.

	\section{Spacecraft Attitude States}
	
	The spacecraft initial attitude is represented using Modified Rodrigues Parameters (MRPs), while the initial angular velocity is expressed in the spacecraft body frame.
	
	The initial spacecraft attitude is defined as:
	
	\[
	\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/N}(t_0) =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0.3 \\
		-0.4 \\
		0.5
	\end{bmatrix}
	\]
	
	The initial body angular velocity is:
	
	\[
	{\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{B/N}(t_0) =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		1.00 \\
		1.75 \\
		-2.20
	\end{bmatrix}
	deg/s
	\]
	
	The spacecraft inertia tensor is assumed diagonal, implying alignment between the body frame and principal inertia axes.	
	\[
	\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{[I]} =
	\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\begin{bmatrix}
			10 & 0 & 0 \\
			0 & 5 & 0 \\
			0 & 0 & 7.5
	\end{bmatrix}}
	\text{ kg·m}^2
	\]	
	
	The spacecraft is assumed to be capable of generating arbitrary three-axis control torques through an onboard actuator system.
	
	\section{Attitude Control Law Overview}
	
	A proportional-derivative (PD) control law is used throughout this project to regulate spacecraft attitude motion:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{u}} = -K\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/R} - P\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/R}
	\end{equation}
	
	where:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/R}$ is the attitude tracking error,
		\item $\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/R}$ is the angular velocity tracking error,
		\item $K$ and $P$ are controller gain matrices.
	\end{itemize}
	
	The controller objective is to asymptotically reduce attitude and angular velocity tracking errors while ensuring stable closed-loop rotational behavior.
	 
	\section{Implementation Methods}
	
	The project was implemented in MATLAB using modular functions for orbital propagation, reference-frame generation, attitude-error evaluation, and closed-loop numerical integration.
	
	The simulation architecture was organized into four primary components:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Orbit propagation functions to compute LMO and GMO inertial states as functions of time,
		\item Reference-frame functions to generate Sun-, nadir-, and GMO-pointing DCMs and angular velocities,
		\item Attitude utilities for MRP/DCM conversion and tracking-error evaluation,
		\item Mission simulation routines to propagate spacecraft dynamics while automatically switching between operational modes.
	\end{itemize}
	
	A fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) integrator was used for numerical propagation of the attitude state. The modular structure simplified validation of individual tasks before integration into the complete mission scenario.
	
	The final MATLAB results were then reconstructed in STK to visually verify spacecraft orientation and reference-frame tracking throughout the mission timeline.
	
	\chapter{Task Descriptions and Solutions}
	
	\section{Orbits}
	
	\subsection{Task 1: Orbit Simulation}
	
	Assume the general orbit frame as $\mathcal{O}: \{\hat{i}_r, \hat{i}_\theta, \hat{i}_h\}$. Even though the general orbit frame and Hill frame basically use the same base vectors, their difference is found in their origin. $\mathcal{O}$ frame has its origin aligned with the origin of inertial frame $\mathcal{N}$ -- which is the center of Mars. The Hill frame $\mathcal{H}$, however, is mounted on the spacecraft, therefore has the same origin as the body frame $\mathcal{B}$. 
	
	Both these frames are obtainable through a (3-1-3) Euler Angle rotation on the inertial frame, though the hill frame origin is also translated to the spacecraft. 
	
	Now, using the $\mathcal{O}$ frame, spacecraft position vector is expressed as:
	$$\prescript{\mathcal{O}}{}{\boldsymbol{r}} = r\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_r$$
	
	Both LMO and GMO orbits are circular, therefore having the same angular velocity $\dot{\theta}$ all over the orbit. Spacecraft velocity vector is expressed as:
	$$\prescript{\mathcal{O}}{}{\boldsymbol{\dot{r}}} = r\dot{\theta}\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_\theta$$
	
	The MATLAB function \texttt{orbitTheta()} takes time, angular velocity and initial angle to calculate the element $\theta$ using the following equation:
	$$\theta_t = \dot{\theta}t + \theta_0$$
		
	Taking the position and velocity vectors from the general orbit frame to the inertial frame is done using the DCM $[NO]$ which can be obtained having all orbital elements at a given time.
	$$[ON] = M_3(\theta_t)M_1(i)M_3(\Omega)$$
	$$[NO] = [ON]^T$$
	$$\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{r}} = [NO] \prescript{\mathcal{O}}{}{\boldsymbol{r}}$$
	$$\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\dot{r}}} = [NO] \prescript{\mathcal{O}}{}{\boldsymbol{\dot{r}}}$$
	
	This is achieved by the MATLAB function \texttt{orbit\_state()}.
	
	
	\subsection{Task 2: Orbit Frame Orientation}
	
	The Hill-frame basis vectors were constructed directly from the inertial position and velocity vectors:
	
	\[
	\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_r} = \frac{\boldsymbol{r}_{LMO}}{\|\boldsymbol{r}_{LMO}\|}
	\]
	
	\[
	\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_h} = \frac{\boldsymbol{r}_{LMO} \times \boldsymbol{\dot{r}}_{LMO}}{\|\boldsymbol{r}_{LMO} \times \boldsymbol{\dot{r}}_{LMO}\|}
	\]
	
	\[
	\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_\theta} = \boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_h \times \boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_r
	\]
	
	To construct a DCM with the base vectors in either frames, we have:
	$$[FB] = 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\prescript{\mathcal{F}}{}{\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}_1} & \prescript{\mathcal{F}}{}{\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}_2} & \prescript{\mathcal{F}}{}{\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}_3}
	\end{bmatrix} =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}_1 ^ T} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}_2 ^ T} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}_3 ^ T}
	\end{bmatrix}	$$
	
	Therefore, to find $[HN]$ we will have:
	$$[HN] = 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_r ^ T} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_\theta ^ T} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_h ^ T}
	\end{bmatrix}$$
	
	A MATLAB function named \texttt{orbit\_frame\_dcm(t)} was implemented to evaluate the time-varying Hill-frame orientation.

	\section{Reference Frame Orientation}
	
	\subsection{Task 3: Sun-Pointing Reference Frame Orientation}
	
	To point the spacecraft solar panels axis $\boldsymbol{\hat{b}}_3$ at the Sun, the reference frame $\mathcal{R}_s$ must be chosen such that $\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_3$ axis points in the Sun direction ($\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_2$ in the scenario). To define the frame completely, the first axis $\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1$ is assumed to point in $-\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_1$ direction. Therefore, the base vectors will be:
	
	$$ \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1 = -\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_1 $$
	$$ \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_3 = \boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_2 $$
	$$ \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_2 = \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_3 \times \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1 = -(\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_2\times\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_1) = \boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_3$$
	
	Similar to the previous task, the analytical expression for the DCM can be obtained using the base vectors:
	
	$$[R_sN] = 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1 ^ T} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_2 ^ T} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_3 ^ T}
	\end{bmatrix}
	= \begin{bmatrix}
		-1 & 0 & 0 \\
		0 & 0 & 1 \\
		0 & 1 & 0
	\end{bmatrix}$$
	
	The resulting reference-frame DCM  can also be obtained using a 3-1-3 Euler-angle of ($\pi$, $\frac{\pi}{2}$, 0) rotation on it. MATLAB function $\texttt{RsN\_DCM()}$ serves this purpose.
	
	Since the base vectors of this frame are just a fixed permutation of inertial frame basis vectors, the frame remains fixed relative to the inertial frame, thus:
	
	$$ \boldsymbol{\omega}_{R_s/N} = 0$$

	
	\subsection{Task 4: Nadir-Pointing Reference Frame Orientation}
	
	To point the spacecraft sensor platform axis $ \boldsymbol{\hat{b}}_1 $ towards the center of Mars (nadir direction), the reference frame $\mathcal{R}_n$ must be chosen such that $ \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1 $ axis points towards the planet. To completely describe the frame, we assume the second axis $ \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_2 $ points in velocity direction $ \boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_\theta $. Therefore, we'll have:
	
	$$  \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1 = -\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_r $$
	$$
	 \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_2 = \boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_\theta 
	$$
	$$ \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_3 = \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1 \times \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_2 = -\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_h$$
	
	This frame can be obtained using a (3-1-3) Euler Angles rotation on the Hill frame with angles ($\pi, \pi, 0$).
	
	$$[R_nH] = M_1(\pi)M_3(\pi)$$
	
	
	 We also had previously obtained $[HN]$ in task 2 as a function of time. So,
	 
	 $$[R_nN] (t) = [R_nH][HN](t) $$
	 
	 In MATLAB, this task is done by function \texttt{RnN\_DCM()}.
	
	Since this frame is a re-arrangement of the Hill frame, it also moves with it, thus:
	$$\boldsymbol{\omega}_{R_n/N} = \boldsymbol{\omega}_{H/N} = \dot{\theta}\boldsymbol{\hat{i}}_\theta$$
	 
	
	
	
	\subsection{Task 5: GMO-Pointing Reference Frame Orientation}
	
	To point the nano-satellite communication platform axis $\boldsymbol{\hat{b}}_1$ towards the GMO mother spacecraft, the communication reference frame $\mathcal{R}_c$ must be chosen such that $-\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1$ axis points towards the GMO satellite location, defining $\Delta\boldsymbol{r} = \boldsymbol{r}_{GMO} - \boldsymbol{r}_{LMO}$. So,
	$$\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1 = -\frac{\Delta\boldsymbol{r}}{\|\Delta\boldsymbol{r}\|}$$
	Further, to fully define a three-dimensional reference frame, assume the second axis is defined as
	$$\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_2 = \frac{\Delta\boldsymbol{r}\times\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_3}{\|\Delta\boldsymbol{r}\times\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_3\|}$$
	The third basis vector is then obtained from
	$$\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_3 = \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1 \times \boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_2$$
	
	Having all three base vectors in the inertial frame, we have:
	$$[R_cN] = 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_1 ^ T} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_2 ^ T} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}_3 ^ T} \\
	\end{bmatrix}$$
	
	The MATLAB function \texttt{RcN\_DCM(t)} is written to deliver this task.
	
	To derive the relative angular velocity $\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{R_c/N}}$, we'll use an equation (3.27) in the textbook \cite{textbook} :
	\begin{equation}
		[\dot{C}] = -[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}][C]
	\end{equation}
	Because the reference-frame motion varies smoothly over time, finite-difference differentiation provides a sufficiently accurate approximation of the frame angular velocity. Therefore,
	\begin{equation}
	[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{R_c/N}] = 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 & -\omega_3 & \omega_2 \\
		\omega_3 & 0 & -\omega_1 \\
		-\omega_2 & \omega_1 & 0
	\end{bmatrix} = -[\dot{R_cN}][R_cN]^T\end{equation}
	
	To derive the rate of a DCM numerically, we'll have:
	\begin{equation}[\dot{R_cN}] \approx \frac{[R_cN]_{t+dt} - [R_cN]_{t}}{dt}\end{equation}
	
	This algorithm is implemented in MATLAB function \texttt{RcN\_omega(t)}. The choice of different \texttt{dt} will result in different precisions in finding the angular velocity.	
	
	\section{Attitude Evaluation and Simulator}
	
	\subsection{Task 6: Attitude Error Evaluation}
	
	Any attitude feedback control algorithm requires the ability to compute the attitude and angular velocity tracking errors of the current body frame $\mathcal{B}$ relative to the reference frame $\mathcal{R}$. 
	To find a set of tracking errors $\begin{bmatrix}
		\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/R} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/R}
	\end{bmatrix}$, we have the known variables $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/N}$, $\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/N}$, as well as the desired reference frame DCM $[RN]$ and frame rate $\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{R/N}$.
	We first construct the DCM $[BN]$ which can be obtained from $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/N}$ using equation (3.152):
	\begin{equation}[C] = [I_{3\times3}] + \frac{8[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}]^2 - 4(1-\sigma^2)[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}]}{(1+\sigma^2)^2}\end{equation}
	
	The inverse transformation--converting a DCM to its MRP form--is formulated in equation (3.153):
	\begin{equation}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}] = \frac{[C]^T - [C]}{\zeta(\zeta+2)} \end{equation}
	$$\zeta = \sqrt{trace([C])+1}$$
	
	These two forms are implemented in MATLAB function \texttt{sigmaToDCM()} and \texttt{DCMToSigma()} respectively. 
	
	Now, the roadmap to finding the tracking errors would be:
	$$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/N} \rightarrow [BN] $$	
	$$[BR] = [BN][NR] = [BN][RN]^T$$
	$$[BR] \rightarrow \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/R}$$
	$$\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/R} = \prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/N} - \prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{R/N} = \prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/N} - [BN]\prescript{\mathcal{N}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{R/N}$$
	
	A MATLAB function named \texttt{attitudeErrorEval()} was implemented to evaluate both attitude and angular velocity tracking errors.
	
	\subsection{Task 7: Numerical Attitude Simulator}
	
	In order to integrate the attitude we need a numerical integrator. The LMO and GMO orbit satellite positions are already calculated in earlier tasks. Let the propagated attitude state $\boldsymbol{X}$ be
	$$\boldsymbol{X} = \begin{bmatrix}
		\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/N} \\
		\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/N}
	\end{bmatrix}$$
	
	Assuming that the spacecraft is rigid and there's no external torque acting on it, its dynamics obey
		
	\begin{equation}
	[I]\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/N} = - [\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/N}][I]\boldsymbol{\omega}_{B/N} + \boldsymbol{u} 
	\end{equation}
	where $\boldsymbol{u}$ is the external control torque vector.
	
	For this task, a loop integrator was written in MATLAB. The control torques $\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{u}}_1 = 0$ and $\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{u}}_2 = (0.01, -0.01, 0.02)$
	were applied respectively and the following were computed:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Attitude MRP $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/N}$
		\item Angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{B/N}$
		\item Angular momentum vector $\boldsymbol{H} = [I]\boldsymbol{\omega}_{B/N}$ in both $\mathcal{N}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ frames
		\item Rotational kinetic energy $T = \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{B/N}^T[I]\boldsymbol{\omega}_{B/N}$
	\end{itemize}
	
	The results of both simulations are plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:task7_results}. As is seen, after applying the control torque $\boldsymbol{u}_2$ on the spacecraft, its rotational kinetic energy increases significantly (from approximately 9 mJ in uncontrolled mode (Figure~\ref{fig:u1_T}) to more than 2.5 J when controlled (Figure~\ref{fig:u2_T})). This is verified by spacecraft angular velocity, whose 1st and 2nd components start to oscillate in controlled mode while its 3rd goes through a rapid, non-stop increase (Figure~\ref{fig:u2_omega}). 
	
	The attitude-state histories further confirm this behavior. The spacecraft exhibits unstable rotational behavior when the control torque $\boldsymbol{u}_2$ is applied to it (Figure~\ref{fig:u2_sigma}). These results indicate that the applied torque does not stabilize the spacecraft attitude dynamics.

	\subsection{Task 8: Sun Pointing Control}
	
	The PD gains were selected using the linearized closed-loop attitude dynamics together with a worst-case inertia design approach. Since scalar gains were used for all spacecraft axes, the gains were chosen based on the maximum and minimum principal moments of inertia to guarantee that all rotational modes satisfy the required 120-second decay time constant. 
	
	$$I_{max} = 10, I_{min} = 5$$
	$$\tau = 120s$$
	
	\begin{equation}K = \frac{4I_{max}^2}{\tau^2I_{min}}, P = \frac{2I_{max}}{\tau}\end{equation}
	
	The control torque is defined as:
	
	\[
	\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{u}} = -K\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{B/R} - P\prescript{\mathcal{B}}{}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{B/R}
	\]
	
	An RK4 integrator was developed to perform numerical propagation of the spacecraft attitude dynamics used by the MATLAB function \texttt{simulate\_mode()}. The results of Sun pointing simulation are evident in Figure~\ref{fig:sun_pointing}.

	\section{Simulation Results and Discussion}
	
	The complete mission simulation demonstrates that the spacecraft attitude-control architecture can autonomously transition between multiple operational pointing modes while maintaining stable closed-loop rotational behavior throughout the mission timeline.
	
	Unlike the previous tasks, where each reference frame remained fixed to a single operational objective, the mission scenario in Task 11 requires the commanded reference frame to vary continuously according to orbital geometry and mission conditions. As a result, the controller must track time-varying attitude commands while responding to discrete transitions between Sun-pointing, communication, and nadir-pointing modes.
	
	The numerical results show that the spacecraft remains dynamically stable throughout the simulation and successfully executes each scheduled mission transition. During mode-switching events, temporary increases in attitude and angular velocity tracking errors occur because of the sudden change in commanded reference orientation. These transient responses are short-lived and are effectively damped by the proportional-derivative control law.
	
	The angular velocity histories further demonstrate that the spacecraft remains detumbled throughout the mission despite repeated reference-frame transitions. Brief angular-rate excursions are observed immediately after switching events as the spacecraft reorients toward the newly commanded reference attitude, but no sustained oscillatory behavior or instability develops.
	
	The simulation also reveals differences in pointing performance between mission modes. Sun-pointing and GMO-pointing exhibit stable convergence and consistent tracking of their commanded orientations. In contrast, the nadir-pointing intervals show slower convergence and incomplete alignment with the commanded Mars-facing reference before the next operational transition occurs. While the controller continues to damp rotational motion and preserve overall stability, the spacecraft does not fully settle into the desired nadir orientation within the available time window.
	
	These results indicate that the implemented control law is effective for autonomous mission execution and spacecraft stabilization, while also highlighting a performance limitation in nadir-pointing operation. This behavior is examined further in the STK visualization chapter, where the three-dimensional mission reconstruction provides additional confirmation of the observed tracking performance.

	\chapter*{Conclusion}
	\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusion}
	
	This project presented the complete development and implementation of an attitude dynamics and control framework for a nano-satellite operating in low Mars orbit. Mission-specific reference frames were derived for Sun-pointing, nadir-pointing, and GMO communication-pointing configurations and integrated into a unified autonomous mission-management architecture.
	
	The spacecraft rotational dynamics, attitude tracking evaluation, and closed-loop proportional-derivative control system were implemented in MATLAB and tested through numerical simulation. The final mission scenario demonstrated that the spacecraft can autonomously transition between operational modes according to orbital conditions while maintaining stable rotational behavior and remaining detumbled throughout the mission.
	
	To further validate the numerical model, the mission was reconstructed in STK and evaluated through three-dimensional visualization. The STK results closely matched the MATLAB simulations and provided direct visual confirmation of spacecraft orientation during each operational segment. Sun-pointing and GMO-pointing modes successfully achieved their intended objectives and showed strong agreement between numerical and visual results.
	
	A key finding of the project was the performance of the nadir-pointing mode. Although the spacecraft remained dynamically stable and continued damping rotational motion, the commanded Mars-facing orientation was not fully achieved within the available segment duration. This behavior was consistently observed in both MATLAB and STK and suggests that additional controller tuning or refinement of the nadir reference-tracking formulation would improve mission performance.
	
	Overall, the project demonstrates the successful integration of spacecraft attitude dynamics, autonomous control, and mission-level visualization for a multi-mode Mars-orbiting nano-satellite. Future work may include higher-fidelity disturbance modeling, actuator and sensor limitations, smoother slew-transition planning between modes, and more advanced nonlinear control strategies.
